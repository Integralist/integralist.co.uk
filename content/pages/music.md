A few bands I keep coming back to, with a quick note on their sound and why
they stuck.

## Cradle of Filth (1992-2000)

Their nineties run (starting with the early demos and running through *The
Principle of Evil Made Flesh*, *Dusk... and Her Embrace*, *Cruelty and the
Beast*, and *Midian*) sits in its own strange corner of UK extreme metal. They
mashed blast beats and Dani Filth's piercing shrieks up against gothic horror
theatrics and twin-guitar harmonies that owed as much to Iron Maiden as to
Scandinavian black metal (alongside sweeping keyboards that gave the whole
thing a Hammer Horror feel). Love them or hate them, they dragged symphonic
black metal out of tape-trading obscurity onto UK festival stages, before later
albums drifted into a slicker gothic metal sound I never quite clicked with.

## SUMAC

Fronted by Aaron Turner (of ISIS and Old Man Gloom, carrying plenty of that
heavy Neurosis-indebted post-metal DNA), with Nick Yacyshyn on drums and Brian
Cook on bass. Where ISIS built slow, hypnotic crescendos, [SUMAC] deliberately
pulls the rug out from under the riffs with jagged time changes and long
passages of raw feedback that feel closer to free jazz than standard metal
songwriting. It is uncomfortable, abrasive music, and it showed post-metal did
not have to settle into a polite formula.

## Sunn O)))

Stephen O'Malley and Greg Anderson named [Sunn O)))] after the Sunn amplifier
brand, which tells you most of what you need to know. There are usually no
drums at all, just glacially slow guitar and bass chords pushed through
towering stacks of tube amps until the overtones and feedback become the song
itself. Treating volume and sustain as the whole composition carried extreme
drone out of metal basements into art galleries and experimental circles
(including an unlikely collaboration with Scott Walker).

## Meshuggah

[Meshuggah] basically rewired modern heavy music. Anchored by Tomas Haake's
mechanical drumming and Fredrik Thordendal's alien, fusion-inflected leads,
their trick is locking shifting polymetric riffs on eight-string guitars
against a steady 4/4 pulse (so even the nastiest parts keep a strange, hypnotic
swing). That rhythmic approach kicked off the entire djent wave, even if most
bands copying their guitar tone seem to miss the cold, patient weight
underneath it.

## Tool

Tool are the rare progressive metal band who kept things awkward and still
topped the album charts. Danny Carey's polyrhythmic drumming and Justin
Chancellor's fuzz-heavy basslines carry most of the weight, with Adam Jones's
textured guitar riffs and Maynard James Keenan's vocals holding back until a
song actually earns the payoff. Records like *Ænima* and *Lateralus* proved a
ten-minute track in an odd time signature could hit just as hard as any radio
single.

## Will Haven

Sacramento noise-metal veterans who never crossed over commercially like their
hometown mates Deftones (who championed them early on), yet quietly influenced
half the heavy hardcore and sludge bands around. From *El Diablo* and *WHVN*
through to *Carpe Diem*, their sound pairs crushed, down-tuned guitar chords
with Grady Avenell's scraped-raw vocals, all locked into pummelling mid-tempo
grooves. Almost nobody else has ever nailed that bleak, cavernous guitar tone.
